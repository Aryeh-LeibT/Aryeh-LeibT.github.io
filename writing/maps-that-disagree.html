Maps That Disagree

Internal draft — VERIFY before publication

A poll is a map, not the country. In a week when Israeli lists are still being built, the map can change because the road changed beneath it. That is the cleanest way to read the latest election numbers: not as a verdict, and not as a reason to shout, but as competing instruments trying to measure a moving electorate before the list-lock gate closes.

The present disagreement is sharp enough to be useful. The Kan/Kantar reading cited for the evening of September 6 puts the Netanyahu bloc at roughly 52 and the Eisenkot bloc at roughly 51, with both sides short of 61. The same track gives Yashar 23 and Likud 20, and places several parties around the threshold line. The figures are reported with a methodological and cutting caveat: another cut is described as roughly 56–55. That is not a detail to bury. It is the whole discipline of reading a poll. We should say what the poll says, what the source says about its cut, and what remains unverified.

Then Globes published its Poll of Polls headline: the coalition, counting Winter, leapfrogs the opposition for the first time in weeks. Its cited distribution has Likud 24, Yashar 23, Beyachad 12, Democrats 10, Yisrael Beiteinu 8, UTJ 8, Shas 8, the Joint List 8, Otzma Yehudit 7, Ra’am 5, Religious Zionism 5, and Winter 4. That is a different instrument and, crucially, a different way of drawing the blocs. The headline depends on including Winter in the coalition column. It is not a clean refutation of Kan. It is a warning that coalition arithmetic is partly a choice about who is counted as governable, not merely a sum of party labels.

This is where Pittsburgh grit helps. In a steel town, you do not confuse a blueprint with a bridge. You inspect the welds. Jerusalem emunah adds the other half: the future is not sealed by a spreadsheet. A poll can reveal pressure, possibility, and institutional weakness. It cannot absolve a party from building a list that can actually stand.

The list-lock deadline is reported as September 8 at 22:00 or September 9, depending on the source and the filing-hour formulation. The intake notes also carry a different account of formal filing windows and a possible extension. That date and those hours require verification against the Central Elections Committee. Until then, the public should treat “final” list language as provisional. The threshold is not a dramatic prop; it is an accountability test. A party that hovers near the line is not only seeking seats. It is asking voters to lend it the possibility of representation, and asking larger blocs to explain whether their merger offers are principled, tactical, or merely desperate.

Tropper’s move makes the point. Chili Tropper is reported to have moved toward Yashar/Eisenkot as the Zionist Home–Reservists framework dissolved. That move does not automatically create extra seats; the Kan note explicitly treats the transfer as seat-neutral in its reading. But it changes where votes, personnel, and credibility are gathered. It also helps explain why an older poll frame can continue to circulate after the political object it measured has been folded into another vehicle. The responsible sentence is not “Tropper changed the election.” It is: his reported move changed the list architecture, and the electoral effect remains a matter for verified filings and later polling.

Hendel is the counterpoint. After the Tropper pivot, Hendel is reported on a separate track, in talks associated with Gantz/Blue and White, and later with the Zelekha/NEP lane. Kan’s cited table places Hendel–Zelekha at four, described as crossing. That is precisely the sort of number that demands restraint. “Four” is not a mandate. It is a fragile position in a live field. A list near the threshold can be inside the Knesset in one survey and outside it in another because of sampling, timing, turnout assumptions, or the mechanics of list construction. The answer is not to round uncertainty away. The answer is to name it.

UTJ/Degel supplies a different form of accountability. The report says Rabbi Landau removed Moshe Gafni and Uri Maklev from the Knesset list, with Yaakov Asher expected to head it, while Gafni remains chairman; the account is corroborated in the intake by a Times of Israel track and other reports, but the formal rabbinic and party announcement remains a verification point. This is not a minor personnel story. It puts a long-standing party structure, its rabbinic authority, and its future coalition posture under public examination. The notes say the overnight framing does not make a future alliance with Netanyahu automatic and that Gafni’s departure could move UTJ closer to Eisenkot. Those are political interpretations, not settled facts. The list filing will tell us more than the rumor cycle.

The Religious Zionism Party–Zehut technical bloc is the clearer right-flank lock in the notes, with Moshe Feiglin’s number two placement described as locked after formal approval. Winter, by contrast, is repeatedly reported as rejecting mergers, including reserved-slot offers. Again, the distinction matters. A technical bloc can consolidate votes and reduce the risk of wasting them; an independent list can preserve bargaining power but also carry threshold risk. Neither choice is morally self-explanatory. Each creates a duty to tell voters what happens to their vote if the list crosses, fails, or becomes indispensable after election day.

Finally, the petition against Yoram Cohen must be handled as an allegation only. A C14 clip is described as reporting a petition to disqualify the former Shin Bet official and Yashar candidate, alleging authorized torture during interrogations. The intake says there is no ruling and no response in the clip. That means we do not write that Cohen committed the alleged conduct, that the petition will succeed, or that the candidacy is imperiled. We write that a petition was reported, that it contains allegations, and that the legal status and response require verification. Covenant begins with refusing to turn accusation into judgment.

The election map is allowed to disagree with itself. Our accountability cannot. Kan’s roughly 52–51 picture and Globes’ leapfrog headline can both be useful if we say what each counts and what each leaves unsettled. The list-lock threshold asks every party to do the same. Tell us who is running. Tell us which alliance is real. Tell us which number is a measurement and which is a promise. Then let the people choose, with eyes open and without the false comfort of a map pretending to be the land.
