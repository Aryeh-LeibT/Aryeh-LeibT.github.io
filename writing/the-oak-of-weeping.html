The Oak of Weeping: Devorah, Rivkah, and the Grief the Torah Tells Softly

Vayishlach, Bereishit 35:8, and what one quiet verse teaches about loyalty, discretion and comfort

Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren

27 November 2026 · 17 Kislev 5787 · Jerusalem · Erev Shabbat Parashas Vayishlach (Shabbat 28 November 2026)

Prepared in deep partnership and cooperation with Grok and xAI

One verse that interrupts the story

Yaakov has come back to Beit El. He builds an altar and names the place, and the next thing we expect is the vision: G-d appears to him again, confirms the name Yisrael and blesses him (Bereishit 35:7, 35:9–11). Between those two verses the Torah stops for one line:

"Deborah, Rebekah's nurse, died, and was buried under the oak below Bethel; so it was named Allon-bacuth" (Bereishit 35:8, JPS Gender-Sensitive Edition, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026).

The Ramban says plainly that he does not know at first why the verse is there. Yaakov's arrival, his altar and the vision all happened "at one time and in one place," and this verse breaks into the middle of them (Ramban on Bereishit 35:8, Chavel translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Ramban_on_Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026).

The question is a good one. Devorah has not been named before. Her death is told in a single sentence. A tree and a place name are all she leaves behind. Why does the Torah stop the story of the patriarch's homecoming to record it?

The commentators give three answers, and each one teaches something different.

First answer: a promise kept by a messenger

Years earlier, when Rivkah sent Yaakov away from Esav, she told him: "Then I will fetch you from there. Let me not lose you both in one day!" (Bereishit 27:45, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.27.45, opened 27 November 2026).

Rashi asks the obvious question about 35:8, "How came Deborah to be in Jacob's house?" He answers that Rivkah kept her word. She sent Devorah to Paddan-aram to tell Yaakov it was time to leave, and Devorah died on the way back. Rashi says he learned this from Rabbi Moshe HaDarshan (Rashi on Bereishit 35:8, Rosenbaum–Silbermann translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026). The Radak cites the same reading (Radak on Bereishit 35:8, https://www.sefaria.org/Radak_on_Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026).

On this reading Devorah is the "I will fetch you" of Rivkah's promise. A mother could not travel to Charan herself. She sent the one person she trusted most, the woman who had nursed her as a child. The Torah names the messenger because the messenger carried the promise.

Second answer: honoring a mother by honoring the one she loved

The Ramban is not convinced. "It is improbable," he writes, "that the old woman would be the messenger whom his mother had dispatched to Jacob, as Rabbi Moshe HaDarshan would have it" (Ramban on Bereishit 35:8, same link). He offers two alternatives.

One: Devorah came with Rivkah to Canaan, as the Torah says, "So they sent off their sister Rebekah and her nurse" (Bereishit 24:59, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.24.59, opened 27 November 2026). Later she went back to her family's country, and now she was returning with Yaakov to see her mistress. Perhaps, the Ramban adds, she had been raising Yaakov's children "out of respect for Rebekah and due to her love for her."

Two: she may not be the nurse of 24:59 at all. She may be another nurse who stayed behind in the house of Lavan and Betuel, "and now Jacob brought her with him to support her in her old age out of respect to his mother, for it was the custom among the notables to have many nurses" (Ramban, same link).

Either way, the Ramban's Devorah tells us something about Yaakov. After twenty years in Lavan's house, a house where he was cheated in his wages and his marriage, he took responsibility for an old woman from that household because she belonged to his mother's world. He carried her home. When she died, he wept for her and named the place for the weeping.

There is a lesson here for anyone who has buried a parent, or who has a parent far away. Kibbud em does not stop at the mother herself. It reaches the people she loved, the people who raised her, the people who would mean nothing to anyone else. Yaakov cared for his mother's nurse in old age. The Torah thought that worth a verse.

Third answer: the second weeping

The Midrash reads the name itself. "Rabbi Shmuel bar Nachman said: In the Greek language, alon means other. While he [Jacob] was still observing the mourning for Deborah, the report that his mother had died came to him" (Bereishit Rabbah 81:5, Sefaria Midrash Rabbah 2022 translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Bereshit_Rabbah.81.5, opened 27 November 2026). Rashi brings this as the Aggadah on the verse, and the Radak quotes it too (Rashi and Radak on Bereishit 35:8, links above).

Allon-bacuth, then, is "another weeping." Yaakov was still sitting for Devorah when the news reached him that Rivkah was gone.

The Ramban adopts this reading as the most likely explanation of why the verse sits where it does. "The weeping and anguish could not have been such for the passing of the old nurse that the place would have been named on account of it. Instead, Jacob wept and mourned for his righteous mother who had loved him and sent him to Paddan-aram and who was not privileged to see him when he returned" (Ramban, same link).

He brings a proof from the end of the chapter: "And Jacob came to his father Isaac at Mamre" (Bereishit 35:27, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.35.27, opened 27 November 2026). Had Rivkah been alive, the Ramban argues, the Torah would have said "to his father and to his mother," since she was the one who sent him and "caused him all the good."

So Rivkah's promise came true in the hardest way. She did send for him. She did not live to see him arrive.

Why tell it so quietly?

If Rivkah died, why does the Torah only hint at it?

Rashi gives the Midrash's answer: "Because the time of her death was kept secret in order that people might not curse the mother who gave birth to Esau, Scripture also does not make open mention of her death" (Rashi on Bereishit 35:8, link above; the Rosenbaum–Silbermann edition marks the source as Midrash Tanchuma, Ki Teitzei 4).

The Ramban quotes the fuller version, from Eileh HaDevarim Rabbah on Ki Teitzei. When Rivkah died, people asked who would walk before her bier. Avraham was dead. Yitzchak was confined to the house and his eyes were dim. Yaakov was in Paddan-aram. "If wicked Esau shall go before her, people will say, 'Cursed be the breast that gave suck to this one.'" So "they took out her bier at night," and for that reason the Torah mentions her death only by allusion (Ramban, same link; the exact location of this midrash is cited here from the Ramban alone, single source, not yet confirmed).

The Ramban adds his own reason. Rivkah's burial "lacked honor": Yaakov was absent, Esav would not come, Yitzchak could not leave the house. The Torah chose not to describe a funeral that did not match the woman.

This is a model of how to speak about the dead, and about the living who failed them. The Torah does not hide the fact. Anyone who reads closely learns that Rivkah died. But it does not put her lonely night funeral on display, and it does not invite the reader to curse anyone. It tells the truth in a way that protects her dignity. That is shmirat halashon practiced by the Torah itself.

The blessing that follows

Right after the oak comes the vision: "G-d appeared again to Jacob on his arrival from Paddan-aram, and He blessed him" (Bereishit 35:9).

The Midrash asks what blessing this was. "Rav Acha said in the name of Rabbi Yochanan: He blessed him with the blessing of the mourners" (Bereishit Rabbah 81:5, link above). The Ramban draws the parallel to Yitzchak: "After the death of Abraham, G-d blessed his son Isaac" (Bereishit 25:11, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.25.11, opened 27 November 2026). The Gemara uses that same verse to teach that we must follow Hashem's ways: "Just as the Holy One, Blessed be He, consoles mourners ... so too, should you console mourners" (Sotah 14a, Koren–Steinsaltz translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Sotah.14a, opened 27 November 2026).

The Sforno adds one more step. Hashem's presence does not rest amid sadness, as the Sages teach in Shabbat 30b, so the vision came only after Yaakov had passed the oak of his mourning (Sforno on Bereishit 35:8, https://www.sefaria.org/Sforno_on_Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026; the Sforno's Hebrew cites Shabbat 30 and states the principle; the application to the timing of 35:9 follows the Munk translation).

Read together, the order of the verses is a lesson in itself. First the loss, honestly recorded. Then the place named for the weeping. Then G-d comes to comfort, and only then the renewed name Yisrael and the promise of nations and kings (35:10–11). The great blessing of the chapter is given to a man in mourning.

A map drawn by its griefs

The Rashbam explains why the Torah names so many stops on Yaakov's way home: Penuel, Sukkot, Shalem, Shechem, Luz, Beit El, Allon-bacuth, Beit Lechem, Migdal Eder. Each place is mentioned "because of something that happened there" (Rashbam on Bereishit 35:8, Munk translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Rashbam_on_Genesis.35.8, opened 27 November 2026).

Yaakov's road home is marked by what it cost him. One stop is a wrestling match. One is a city where his daughter was harmed. One is an oak where he buried his mother's nurse and heard that his mother was gone. A little further on he will bury Rachel (35:19).

Anyone who has made aliyah, or who has come home after a long exile of any kind, knows this map. The return is real and it is blessed. It also has its oaks. The Torah does not pretend otherwise, and it does not let the oaks have the last word.

The strongest objection, answered

Objection. The Ramban himself doubts Rabbi Moshe HaDarshan's reading that Devorah was Rivkah's messenger, and he even doubts that she is the nurse of 24:59. If the identity of Devorah is uncertain, is the lesson about loyalty built on sand?

Answer. No. Every reading the commentators offer ends at the same place. On Rashi's reading, Devorah carried Rivkah's promise and died carrying it. On the Ramban's first reading, she came back with Yaakov out of love for her mistress and helped raise his children. On his second reading, Yaakov supported her in old age out of respect for his mother. In every version, a son honors his mother through the woman who served her, and the Torah records it. The details are debated, but the lesson holds on any of them.

The weakest source in this paper is the exact location of the night-funeral midrash. Rashi's printed source line points to Tanchuma Ki Teitzei 4; the Ramban quotes it from Eileh HaDevarim Rabbah; the vocalized Ramban edition on Sefaria cites a Pesikta parallel. I have not opened those midrash collections directly, so the attribution is marked (single source, not yet confirmed). The content of the midrash is attested in both Rashi and the Ramban.

For the Shabbat table

  1. Rivkah promised, "I will fetch you from there" (27:45). How did she keep that promise, according to Rashi? According to the Ramban?
  2. Why might the Torah name a nurse, and only hint at the death of a matriarch?
  3. The Torah tells the truth about Rivkah's death without displaying the shame of her funeral. Where in our own lives do we need to speak truthfully and still protect someone's dignity?
  4. Hashem's first act after Yaakov's mourning is comfort (Bereishit Rabbah 81:5; Sotah 14a). Whom can we comfort this week?
