Not by Your Righteousness: Bitul, the Quill of the Heart, and the Human Bridge on the Eve of Tu B’Av

28 July 2026 · Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren

Originally written 28 July 2026 (14 Av 5786). Archive: Parsha Eikev · Daily Learning.

The calendar of 14 Av 5786 places the learner in a luminous yet understated interval. The fast of Tisha B’Av has passed; the consolatory haftarot of Nachamu have begun their work of comfort. On 15 Av (Wednesday, 29 July 2026) the full moon of Tu B’Av rises, carrying with it the Talmudic memory of the day on which the tribes were permitted to intermarry, the day the last of the wilderness generation ceased to die, and the day the daughters of Israel went out to dance in the vineyards (Ta’anit 26b, 30b–31a). Between the mourning that has been set aside and the joy that is about to intensify stands this ordinary Tuesday, whose daily portions form a precise curriculum of preparation.

The Chumash reading is the third aliyah of Parshat Eikev (Devarim 9:4–29). The Tehillim cycle is chapters 72–76. The Tanya is the middle of Iggeret HaKodesh, Epistle 5. The Rambam covers Hilchot Avodat Yom ha-Kippurim, chapters 4 and 5, and Hilchot Me’ilah, chapter 1. The Hayom Yom records the omission of Tachanun at Mincha and transmits a teaching of the Alter Rebbe concerning the “quill of the heart.” The daily daf touches the mitzvah of tzitzit and its hidden redemptive force (Chullin 89). The Shir Shel Yom is Tehillim 82. And the daily thought of the Chabad calendar declares that the human being is G-d’s bridge between heaven and earth.

Taken together, these texts do not merely instruct; they form. They insist upon bitul—the nullification of the claim that inheritance or standing rests upon native righteousness. They demand that learning pass through the heart. They locate justice under a higher tribunal. They reveal the power of concealed fidelity. They define the human vocation as mediation between the Infinite and the finite. And they do so on the very threshold of a festival whose essence is covenantal love and the healing of internal division.

This essay undertakes a sustained reading of these portions, moving from close exegesis to thematic development, and then to a careful synthesis with the language of covenantal republican political theory—particularly the ideals of non-domination, formative civic education, and the cultivation of character through ritual and habit. The aim is neither to reduce Torah to political philosophy nor to baptize secular republicanism, but to allow the theological claims of the daily learning to speak in a register that illuminates their public and formative consequences, while remaining anchored in the primacy of Divine sovereignty and the eternal bond of the Jewish soul.

I. “Not by Your Righteousness”: Exegesis of Devarim 9:4–29

Moshe Rabbeinu opens the section with a direct address to the interior disposition of the people as they stand on the threshold of the Land:

“Do not say in your heart, after the Lord your G-d has thrust them out before you, saying: ‘It is because of my righteousness that the Lord has brought me in to possess this land’; whereas it is because of the wickedness of these nations that the Lord is driving them out before you. Not by your righteousness or by the uprightness of your heart do you come to possess their land, but because of the wickedness of these nations the Lord your G-d drives them out from before you, and in order to fulfill the word that the Lord swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.” (Devarim 9:4–5)

The language is deliberately psychological. The danger is not merely external triumphalism but an interior narrative of self-justification. Rashi, following the Sifrei, emphasizes that the conquest is an act of judgment against the Canaanite nations and of covenantal loyalty to the Patriarchs. The people are warned against constructing a story in which their own moral superiority is the decisive cause. Ramban develops the point further: after victory, the natural human tendency is to attribute success to one’s own power and virtue. The Torah anticipates this and cuts it off at the root.

The narrative then pivots, almost without pause, to the Golden Calf (9:7–21). The chronological juxtaposition is theologically charged. Immediately after forbidding the claim of righteousness, Moshe recounts the paradigmatic national failure. The people who are about to inherit the Land are the descendants of those who, within weeks of the revelation at Sinai, fashioned a molten image and declared, “These are your gods, O Israel.” The first tablets, written by the finger of G-d, were shattered. Moshe’s forty days and nights of intercession, the grinding of the calf to powder, and the eventual gift of the second tablets constitute the deepest commentary on the opening warning.

The second tablets are not a mere replacement. They are given after sin and after pleading. They embody a covenant that has already absorbed rupture and restoration. Midrashic and later Chassidic readings often note that the broken first tablets were preserved in the Ark together with the intact second set. The relationship includes both the ideal of unbroken fidelity and the reality of human failure met by Divine compassion. Bitul, in this light, is not self-hatred; it is the recognition that the covenant itself is sustained by a faithfulness that exceeds human desert.

Chassidic interpretation, particularly in the school of Chabad, intensifies the psychological and ontological dimensions of this bitul. The claim “by my righteousness” is a subtle form of yeshut—self-assertion that imagines an independent standing before G-d. True avodah begins when that claim is relinquished. The inheritance of the Land, the reception of Torah, and even the capacity for teshuvah are gifts. The second tablets, received after Moshe’s self-nullifying intercession on behalf of the people, become the model: leadership and learning that proceed from bitul rather than from the assertion of personal merit.

The section closes with Moshe’s further forty days of prayer and the appointment of the Levites (9:22–29). The overall arc is clear: from the prohibition of self-congratulation, through the memory of national sin, to the demonstration of sustained intercessory bitul, and finally to the institutional continuity of the Levitical service. The Torah does not leave the people in a state of permanent self-accusation; it moves them through the memory of failure into a renewed covenantal order grounded in Divine initiative.

II. The Quill of the Heart: Hayom Yom and the Interiorization of Learning

The Hayom Yom for 14 Av records two related facts. First, Tachanun is not recited at Mincha. Second, it transmits a teaching connected to the beginning of the Tzemach Tzedek’s literary activity:

When the Tzemach Tzedek began writing on Talmudic and Chassidic subjects, the Alter Rebbe told him: “Uknei lekha chaver” (Avot 1:6)—read it as “Ve-kaneh lekha chaver,” “the quill shall be your friend.” My father [the Rebbe Rayatz] explained: This refers to the quill of the heart. Whatever one learns must be experienced emotionally.

The omission of Tachanun already signals a shift in the spiritual atmosphere. The community is moving from the mode of petition and self-scrutiny that characterizes the days after Tisha B’Av toward the anticipatory joy of Tu B’Av. Yet the teaching about the quill insists that this movement cannot be merely external or calendrical. Learning itself must undergo a transformation.

The Alter Rebbe’s instruction is characteristically precise. The acquisition of a “friend” is reinterpreted as the acquisition of an instrument of interiorization. The quill is not merely a tool for recording; it is the means by which the intellect’s grasp is pressed into the heart’s experience. In Chabad terminology, this is the movement from haskalah (intellectual understanding) to feeling, and ultimately to the transformation of middot. Torah that remains only in the sechel is incomplete. It must become the animating force of ahavah and yirah.

This demand has profound implications for chinuch and for adult avodah alike. A curriculum that transmits information without engaging the heart produces scholars who may be able to argue but who are not necessarily changed. The “quill of the heart” is the Chassidic insistence that authentic learning is formative. It shapes disposition, reorients desire, and generates the emotional energy required for sustained service. On the eve of a festival whose essence is love and communal rejoicing, the teaching is particularly apt: the joy of Tu B’Av is not sentimental; it is the fruit of hearts that have been trained to feel the reality of covenant.

III. Justice under the Divine Gaze and the Power of Hidden Merit

Tehillim 72–76 form a sequence concerned with righteous kingship, the endurance of G-d’s name, the memory of past deliverance, and the ultimate judgment of the nations. The Shir Shel Yom of Tuesday, Tehillim 82, crystallizes the theme:

“G-d stands in the divine assembly; in the midst of the judges He judges. How long will you judge unjustly and show partiality to the wicked? … I said, ‘You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you’; nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince.” (Tehillim 82:1–2, 6–7)

The psalm addresses those who exercise judicial or governmental power. Their authority is real, yet it is derivative and accountable. The claim “you are gods” is immediately qualified by the reminder of mortality and judgment. Human courts are not autonomous; they stand under the gaze of the One who stands in the assembly.

This insistence finds a parallel in the Talmudic discussion of Chullin 89 concerning the mitzvah of tzitzit. The generation of the wilderness was saved, in part, by the merit of this commandment. The blue thread, the almost invisible detail of the garment, possesses redemptive force that is disproportionate to its apparent size. Merit is often concealed. The scales of heaven are moved by acts that do not attract public notice. In an age that measures value by visibility and metrics, the sugya is a quiet corrective.

The Rambam’s simultaneous study of the Yom Kippur service and the laws of me’ilah sharpens the boundary around the sacred. The elaborate choreography of the High Priest’s day, the multiple immersions, the precise order of offerings, and the confession over the scapegoat all insist that approach to the holy is possible yet strictly ordered. Me’ilah, the prohibition against the unauthorized use of consecrated property, protects the distinction between what has been dedicated to G-d and what remains within the ordinary human domain. Together these laws teach that human agency in the realm of the sacred is genuine, responsible, and limited. One may serve; one may not appropriate.

IV. The Soul’s Eternal Bond and the Human as Bridge

Iggeret HaKodesh, Epistle 5, in its middle section, develops the Alter Rebbe’s teaching on the soul’s root in the Divine and the capacity of even the simplest Jewish act to draw down infinite light. The soul is never severed from its source. Every mitzvah, however modest in external appearance, becomes a channel through which the Infinite is drawn into the finite. This teaching gathers the preceding themes: the inheritance is not earned by righteousness, yet the soul remains bound; learning must reach the heart; judgment belongs to G-d; hidden strings of fidelity sustain the world.

The daily thought of the Chabad calendar states the anthropological conclusion with characteristic clarity:

The human being is G-d’s bridge between heaven and earth. Animals do not gaze in wonder at the stars and angels are confined to the realm of the spirit, but the human being is G-d’s bridge between heaven and earth. Our bodies are formed from the dust; our souls are of the essence of G-d. We alone can look at a physical world and see spiritual life and beauty. We who are beyond both heaven and earth, form and matter, spirit and body—we alone can fuse the two.

This is not a vague spirituality. It is a precise description of the covenantal task. The human person is the point of contact. Material objects, daily routines, professional responsibilities, and interpersonal relationships are not neutral; they are the very materials that can be elevated or left unredeemed. The bridge is crossed in both directions: heaven is drawn into earth through intentional action, and earth is lifted toward heaven through the same action performed with awareness of its Source.

V. Synthesis: Bitul, Formative Practice, and Covenantal Republicanism

The theological claims of the day’s learning admit of a fruitful conversation with the tradition of civic republican thought that emphasizes non-domination, the formation of character, and the role of ritual and habit in sustaining free political life.

Philip Pettit’s articulation of freedom as non-domination—the condition of not being subject to the arbitrary will of another—finds a theological analogue in the Torah’s repeated warning against the self that imagines its power and standing as self-generated. A citizenry that believes its achievements are solely the product of its own righteousness is already halfway to the arrogance that produces domination. Bitul, properly understood, is the recognition that all power is held in trust under a higher sovereignty. Such recognition does not produce passivity; it produces the humility that makes responsible agency possible and the vigilance that resists both personal and institutional overreach.

The demand that learning pass through the heart parallels the republican emphasis on formative education and civic virtue. Institutions that transmit only technical knowledge leave the deeper springs of action untouched. The “quill of the heart” is the insistence that authentic formation engages emotion, desire, and disposition. Tu B’Av itself, with its historical permission for intermarriage among the tribes and its association with communal joy after the long decree of the wilderness, offers an image of civic friendship and the healing of internal divisions. Republican thought has long valued concord and the common good; the festival supplies a Jewish ritual expression of the same aspiration under the explicit canopy of covenant.

The human as bridge further resonates with the understanding of the citizen as the living medium in which transcendent moral order and concrete political life meet. Neither pure otherworldliness nor pure immanence suffices. The citizen who can elevate the material through intention, who can act justly where action is possible and trust where it is not, and who can remember that even the smallest fidelity may tip unseen scales, becomes the practical embodiment of a healthy public order.

Safeguards must be maintained. Theological grounding in Divine sovereignty, the eternal bond of the soul, and the priority of bitachon prevents the slide into the pathologies that can afflict secular republicanism: statism, majoritarian tyranny, or nostalgic communitarianism without transcendence. Chassidic emphasis on invitational rather than coercive chinuch further protects against authoritarian pedagogy. The synthesis is therefore asymmetrical: the Torah remains the source and the measure; republican categories supply a language for articulating public consequences.

VI. The Practice of the Day

The portions of 14 Av invite concrete disciplines. One may keep a private record of achievements and, each evening, acknowledge their Source. One may pause after study and ask whether the heart has been moved, and if not, return to the text until some spark of feeling appears. One may perform one act of justice or of concealed fidelity—repair of a relationship, careful observance of a boundary around the sacred, a quiet intention placed upon an ordinary object—and trust that the scales are affected. One may regard body, speech, tools, and professional responsibilities as materials to be elevated rather than as private property. In these ways the bridge is traversed daily.

In an age marked by both exaggerated claims of self-authorship and deep public distrust of institutions, the day’s learning offers a double corrective. It refuses the narrative of pure self-making. It also refuses the despair that concludes that nothing ordinary can matter. The hidden blue thread, the second tablets given after failure, the heart that learns to feel, and the human who stands as bridge together constitute a curriculum of sober hope.

Conclusion: The Quiet Preparation

The day before Tu B’Av is not an empty interval. It is the space in which bitul is renewed, the heart is trained to receive, justice is remembered under the Divine gaze, hidden merits are valued, and the human vocation as bridge is accepted afresh. The consolations of Nachamu continue to sound; the full joy of the fifteenth of Av approaches. Between them stands the work of this day: to receive the inheritance not as earned possession but as covenantal gift, to learn with the quill of the heart, and to stand, in body and soul, as the living point of contact between the Infinite and the world that still awaits its complete redemption.

May the learning of this day strengthen the quiet bridges upon which a more faithful life—personal, communal, and public—can be built.
