Who Is a Jew — Status, Halacha, Heresy, and the Light That Remains

29 Elul 5786 · 11 September 2026 (Erev RH 5787)

Companion clarification to Yisrael Af Al Pi Shechata — A Jew Remains a Jew, and the Covering Has a Name.

People keep arguing about who is a Jew as if the noun were a mood, a coalition, or a costume you can put on for a brief and take off when the politics turn. It is not. Covenant is older than the news cycle. The brit does not wait for your comfort, and it does not dissolve because someone finds the categories inconvenient.

This is a public clarification for people who do not get it — in the Diaspora and in Israel alike. It is not a hit piece. It is not a party platform. It refuses to flatten politics into definition. Hold four distinctions carefully, with their classical sources in view, and most of the confusion collapses.

1. Status: yes or no — no “almost”

A Jew is a Jew because his mother is a kosher Jew, or because he truly converted through a rabbinical Bet Din.

That is the gate. Yes or no. There is no “almost Jew.”

Maternal lineage. The Gemara in Kiddushin 68b reads Devarim 7:3–4 — “for he will turn away your son from following Me” — and states the principle in Rabbi Yoḥanan’s name from Rabbi Shimon ben Yoḥai: your son from a Jewish woman is called “your son”; your son from a gentile woman is not called your son, but her son. Status follows the mother. Rambam codifies the same general rule in Hilchot Issurei Biah 15:4: when a child is born from a gentile or a maidservant, “he is like his mother”; “we are not concerned with the father.” Earlier in the same book he contrasts this with other forbidden unions: even a mamzer is “considered a member of the Jewish people,” whereas a son conceived by a gentile woman “is not considered his son” (12:7, reading Devarim 7:4). Shulchan Aruch Even HaEzer 8:5 states the same direction of travel for the child of a non-Jewish woman or maidservant: the child retains her status.

True conversion. The other gate is gerut. Rambam (Issurei Biah 13:1–4) teaches that entry into the covenant requires circumcision, immersion, and — in Temple times — sacrifice; for future generations a gentile who wishes to “enter into the covenant, take shelter under the wings of the Divine Presence, and accept the yoke of the Torah” must undergo circumcision and immersion (sacrifice awaiting the Temple). Acceptance of the yoke is not optional ornament. Shulchan Aruch Yoreh Deah 268 requires that matters of the convert — informing of mitzvot, receiving them, circumcision, immersion — be done with three who are fit to judge, by day; and it is explicit that kabbalat hamitzvot before a qualified court is indispensable. A certificate that treats Sinai as optional branding is not that gate. A feeling is not that gate.

Not a vibe. Not a surname. Not “I feel Jewish.” Not “my father was Jewish and that should count.” Kindness, politics, and pain do not rewrite lineage. The noun is not a membership card issued by the Right and cancelled by the Left, or the reverse. It is the people’s gate, received and guarded.

Someone born of a Jewish mother who never opened a siddur is still a Jew by status. Someone who did not pass that gate — and did not complete a true Bet Din conversion — is not. Mercy without definition is not mercy; it is erosion.

2. Living by the Jewish way: Halacha, via the Thirteen Principles

Status answers who is inside the people. It does not yet answer how one lives as a Jew.

The only way to live by the Jewish way is through Halacha — and Halacha is not a buffet. Its backbone of belief is clear in the Thirteen Principles of Faith as Rambam formulated them in his Commentary on the Mishnah to Sanhedrin 10:1 (the introduction to Perek Chelek): G-d’s existence and unity; that He is not a body; that He is first; that He alone is rightly worshipped; prophecy; the prophecy of Moshe; Torah from Heaven; Torah’s immutability; that G-d knows the deeds of men; reward and punishment; the coming of Mashiach; and the resurrection of the dead.

Rambam is careful about what belief does. After listing the thirteen, he writes that one who believes in all of them “enters into the category of Israel” for the commandments of love and brotherhood — and even if such a person sins from desire, he is punished according to his sins but remains among the sinners of Israel with a share in the World to Come. That sentence already separates fidelity of foundations from sin of appetite. Culture, memory, and solidarity against hatred are precious and obligatory. None of them substitutes for Halacha as the way of Jewish life, and none rewrites the Principles into a mood of the age.

A Jew may be distant, wounded, ignorant, or angry and still be addressable for return. What he may not do is redefine the road itself and then demand that Sinai salute the redefinition. Living by the Jewish way means mitzvot, not metaphors. The Name binds even when the coalition does not clap. Atem nitzavim hayom kulchem (Devarim 29:9) was said under a brit, not under a brand.

3. Denial: still a Jew — and a heretic

Here the categories must not be collapsed.

Whoever denies this — whoever rejects maternal status as Halacha defines it, or rejects that the Jewish way runs through Halacha and the Principles — is still a Jew if he is a Jew by status. He is a heretic. Those are different sentences. Mix them and you get either cheap excommunication or cheap absolution.

Heresy named. Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1 opens with the mercy — “All of the Jewish people have a share in the World-to-Come” — and then names exceptions: one who denies resurrection from the Torah, one who says the Torah is not from Heaven, and an epikoros. Rambam’s code in Hilchot Teshuvah 3:6–8 sorts the vocabulary with severity: minim; apikorsim (deniers of prophecy, of Moshe’s prophecy, or of G-d’s knowledge of human deeds); and those who deny the Torah (even one verse as not from Heaven; denial of Oral Torah; claim that the Torah has been replaced). In the Commentary on the Mishnah he is still sharper: compromise of a Principle and the person is called apostate, heretic, “one who cuts the plantings.”

Status not revoked. That severity is about belief, Olam HaBa, and covenantal standing in the faith-sense. It is not a license to un-Jew the noun for someone who entered by maternal lineage or true gerut. Sanhedrin 44a closes the cheaper door: on the verse “Israel has sinned” (Joshua 7:11), Rabbi Abba bar Zavda teaches that even when they have sinned, they are still called Israel — like a myrtle among thorns that is still called myrtle. The companion essay in this cluster said it plainly: keep the noun; name the covering; do not pretend the covering is fidelity. Yisrael af al pi shechata, Yisrael hu.

So hold the registers apart. Jew by status (mother or Bet Din). Heretic / apikores / kofer by denial of foundations. Living by Halacha — or not. Three registers. Rambam’s “exits the category of Israel” in the Ikkarim names rupture in faith-community standing and World-to-Come claim; Sanhedrin 44a and the practical gate of lineage refuse to turn that rupture into a gentile redo. A heretic remains claimable by the people and accountable before Heaven. He does not get to resign the noun when it becomes inconvenient, and we do not get to revoke it when we are angry. Severity without the noun is a different religion’s move. Mercy without naming heresy is flattery.

4. Neshama: the light remains under the covering

All Jews have a neshama. That is not sentiment.

Tanya, Likkutei Amarim chapter 1, following R. Chaim Vital, teaches that in every Jew — whether righteous or wicked — there are two souls. Chapter 2 states the second soul of a Jew is “truly a part of G-d above” (chelek Eloka mima’al mamash), as it is written, “He breathed into his nostrils a soul of life.” The nefesh Elokit is not issued for good behaviour and cancelled for bad. The kelipah can thicken until the spark is inaudible to the man himself. Sins cover the light. Ideology can bury it under five feet of cleverness. The spark does not leave.

That is why “ham sandwich” theology fails even when the insult lands. Tell a Jew he is not a Jew and — as I have written — the neshama yells from within. The yell is not a policy preference. It is the refusal of the nefesh Elokit to be cancelled by a punchline. The work is not un-Jewing. The work is uncovering.

Yisrael af al pi shechata and “a Jew remains a Jew” are the same mercy and the same severity. They mean the covering has a name, and the door of teshuvah stays open in principle without pretending that denial is fidelity or that status is a lifestyle. The companion piece — Yisrael Af Al Pi Shechata — A Jew Remains a Jew, and the Covering Has a Name — already dug a live case under that heading. This clarification does not twin that dig. It states the definition those pages presuppose. Elul language — and we write this on the threshold of a year — is exact here: the King is in the field, including the field of a soul under a thick shell. Reception is not flattery. Accounting remains.

What this refuses

It refuses exporting a family fight into the definition itself. Bitter argument about governments and borders does not rewrite who counts as a Jew. Non-domination means no office and no faction gets arbitrary power to pick which Jews count as conscience — and no faction gets to dissolve the gate because the gate is hard. Categories first; persons are not punchlines. Dig when a case requires digging. The unencumbered self that wants Jewishness as a portable moral license — pick it up at the microphone, set it down before Maariv — is refused. Thick identity does not work that way.

Noun: Jew.

Covering: sin, ideology, denial.

And not: redefine the gate until nothing remains.

That is the clarification. Hold it without glibness. Teach it without turning covenant into content. From Yerushalayim and from every intact Jewish table, the task is the older one: guard the definition, keep the noun, name heresy where belief has ruptured, and leave return open without lying about the light under the shell.
