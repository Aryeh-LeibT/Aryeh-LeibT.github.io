Who Decides When the Threat Is "Immediate"? Vayishlach and the Caretaker Gap

Gift, prayer and readiness for war, and the republic that has to choose among them while its government is still being formed

Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren

24 November 2026 · 14 Kislev 5787 · Jerusalem · in the week of Parashas Vayishlach (Shabbat 28 November 2026)

Prepared in deep partnership and cooperation with Grok and xAI

The sentence at the center

On Monday, 23 November 2026, within minutes of each other, two Israeli leaders said opposite things about the same danger. Gadi Eisenkot, the Yashar chairman who has been tasked with forming the next government, said at his faction meeting: "Regarding Iran, I do not currently see a direct and immediate threat to the State of Israel that requires action before a government is formed. The most serious threat from Iran is those 440 kilograms of enriched uranium that have still not been destroyed or removed. We need to focus on them" (Israel National News, 23 November 2026, 3:23 PM). Shortly afterward, at the Likud faction meeting, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu said: "Anyone who dismisses or downplays these threats" does so "for political reasons," and "This is real, this is serious." Ynetnews put it in its headline: "The threat from Iran is immediate." AFP reported both statements as well, with Netanyahu's wording translated slightly differently ("belittles or downplays these immediate, serious and grave threats").

This paper is not about which of them is right. I cannot see the intelligence, and neither can you. It is about a question that both statements leave open: who has the standing to judge that a threat is "immediate," and who decides on war, in the weeks when the government that would own the consequences is still being formed? I will argue the principle and leave the people out of it. Each man is doing what his office and his responsibility push him to do, and each has a serious argument. The principle is larger than both.

What each side actually claimed

Read the quotes again. Eisenkot did not say Iran is harmless. He called Iran's threat the gravest one facing the State (Middle East Eye, 23 November 2026), and he named the 440 kilograms of enriched uranium as the real problem. His claim is narrower: nothing so immediate that it cannot wait for a government. Netanyahu's claim is that the threat is real and that those who minimize it speak politically. Notice the shape of the exchange. Each statement carries an accusation about motive inside it, or invites one back. Motive is the one thing an outsider cannot inspect. A republic that settles a question of war by weighing the motives of whoever spoke last has lost its footing.

Three further items sit around the dispute, and each is a claim and nothing more.

First, Times of Israel reported on 22 November 2026, citing Channel 12, that IDF Military Intelligence officials warned, at a closed-door meeting last week attended by the prime minister and senior defense officials, that an attack on Iran could ignite "multiple fronts," involving Lebanon, Syria and Gaza, against expectations that an operation could stay limited to three to five days. This is a report of a closed-door meeting, the IDF declined to comment, and I mark it as reported, not confirmed.

Second, former Mossad chief David Barnea told the ELNET summit in Jerusalem on 23 November 2026 that Israel must "act swiftly and decisively to bring about the downfall of this regime," even if "that means going to another war," and that the time to launch is "as soon as all conditions allow" (Times of Israel; i24NEWS headlines it "even at the cost of war"). He is a former official. His view is an input to the debate and carries no authority to decide it.

Third, Times of Israel reported that President Trump told Fox News on 22 November 2026 that he is "inclined to launch another offensive against Iran if a deal is not reached by the new year." Eisenkot's deadline to form a government is 6 December 2026, extendable by 14 days (AFP). If those two reports are right, the American clock may run past the coalition clock. That matters for the argument below, because it means the gap may close before any decision is forced.

Yaakov: three preparations, in order

Parashas Vayishlach opens with Yaakov afraid of his brother's approach. The verse says "Jacob was greatly frightened; in his anxiety, he divided the people with him" into two camps (Bereishit 32:8, JPS). He then says, "If Esau comes to the one camp and attacks it, the other camp may yet escape" (32:9, JPS). Rashi on 32:9 reads the whole chapter as a plan: "He prepared himself for three things," a gift, prayer and war (Rashi, translation of Rosenbaum and Silbermann). The gift is the present that "passed before him." The prayer is Yaakov's plea to the G-d of his father Avraham. The war preparation is the division into two camps, so that if one is struck the other survives.

Three things in that structure bear on the caretaker gap.

First, readiness is not postponed. Yaakov does not wait for certainty about Esav's intent before dividing his camp. A republic in a caretaker period should likewise keep its defenses, its warnings and its diplomacy fully running. Nobody in this debate argues otherwise, but the phrase "before a government is formed" can be misheard as a call to do nothing until then, and Yaakov would not have said that.

Second, war comes last. The gift and the prayer come before the camp is divided for battle, and the verse's own language about war is cautious: the aim is that "the other camp may yet escape." Yaakov's war plan is built around limiting loss if he is wrong. That is the question the IDF warning raises, whatever its final accuracy: if a strike goes badly and fronts multiply, what is the surviving camp, and who has planned for it?

Third, and here I must be honest about the limits of the parallel: Yaakov was a patriarch deciding for his own household. Nobody had to authorize him. A republic is a different thing, and the Torah itself has a separate teaching for it.

The Rambam: the classification belongs to an institution

The Rambam divides wars into two kinds. A king fights first a milchemet mitzvah, which includes "a war fought to assist Israel from an enemy which attacks them" (Mishneh Torah, Hilchot Melachim 5:1, translation of Rabbi Eliyahu Touger). Only afterward may he fight a milchemet hareshut, an optional war. The consequence is in 5:2: "There is no need to seek the permission of the court to wage a milchemet mitzvah." But for an optional war, "he may not lead the nation out to wage a milchemat hareshut unless the court of seventy one judges approves." In Hebrew: אֲבָל מִלְחֶמֶת הָרְשׁוּת אֵינוֹ מוֹצִיא הָעָם בָּהּ אֶלָּא עַל פִּי בֵּית דִּין שֶׁל שִׁבְעִים וְאֶחָד. The Mishnah says the same in Sanhedrin 1:5, "only on the basis of a court of seventy-one judges" (William Davidson edition).

Read this as a structure, not a verdict. When the war is truly defensive, the king moves at once, with no court, because delay would cost lives. When the war is optional, the king does not decide alone. Both halves are about speed against accountability. And the line between the two halves is exactly the question the news is asking in modern words. When Eisenkot says he sees no immediate threat, he is saying: this is not yet a war of assisting Israel against an enemy who has come upon it. When Netanyahu says the threat is real and serious, he is saying the opposite. The word "immediate" is doing the work of the Rambam's classification, and nobody in the public debate has said which category an enemy with an unaccounted-for stockpile belongs to. Where a preemptive strike falls between these two categories is a question for rabbis who rule on it, and I do not presume to answer it here.

What I can say is what the structure teaches. Classification of a war is never left to the single voice that wants to fight it. The Torah's own answer assigns the classification of the contested case to a court with the standing and the information, and I note that there is no Sanhedrin today. The republic's equivalent is institutional: a government decision rather than a personal one. The Israel Democracy Institute explains that under section 40(a) of Basic Law: The Government, the authority to decide whether to launch a war lies with the government, and so does the authority over "a significant military operation which is liable to lead, at a level of probability close to certainty, to war." It can be delegated to the political-security cabinet (March 2, 2026). I did not find a source on whether caretaker status changes any of this, and I do not claim it does. That is a question for jurists, and it is exactly the question the next weeks will press.

The caretaker gap, and the substance underneath it

A caretaker period creates a particular risk. The government in office holds the intelligence. The one who is tasked with replacing it does not hold it, but may inherit the result. A decision made in the gap lands on someone who did not make it. So here is the principle. The more irreversible the act, the wider the circle that must stand behind it, and the less it can rest on one person's reading of the information. Reversible steps, such as raising readiness, quiet diplomacy and prayer, need no waiting. An act that cannot be undone, which is what a war is, calls for the whole structure the Rambam describes: a body that sees the evidence and is answerable for it.

The 440 kilograms is the substance, and it deserves its own sentence. Eisenkot himself named it as the most serious threat and said the focus should be there. That is a hard, physical, accountable matter. It does not turn on anyone's tone at a faction meeting. It will still be there on 6 December and on 1 January. A threat of that kind argues for a standing process that does not depend on who happens to be speaking, so that the question of whether it is immediate is answered by a defined forum with the evidence in front of it, and answered the same way whichever bloc is leading.

The counterweight: air power and the regime

Prof. Kobi Michael of INSS and the Misgav Institute, speaking to ILTV on 23 November 2026, said that he is not sure an air campaign alone would topple the Iranian regime, that a ground operation would be needed for that, and that he hopes a campaign would at least weaken the regime enough to bring it back to the negotiating table. (This is from the interview's automatic captions, which I read for these passages. His introduction is at about 03:12, and the passage on air power runs from about 08:43 to 09:15. The timestamp of 03:50 in the earlier summary was where he began answering a different question, on red lines, so I have not used it.)

This counterweight is useful to the argument in two ways. It separates two goals that the loud version of the debate merges. Removing the uranium and the missile capacity is one goal. Toppling a regime is another, and Prof. Michael doubts the second can be done from the air. Barnea's call to "bring about the downfall of this regime" speaks to the second. Eisenkot's focus on the 440 kilograms speaks to the first. Which goal a strike serves also determines which of the Rambam's categories it belongs to, so the debate about "immediate" is really a debate about purpose. He also warned that a third round resembling the previous two would achieve almost nothing. A republic about to decide on a third round owes its citizens a clear answer to what it is for.

The strongest objection

The strongest objection says that all of this describes a luxury. Real threats do not wait for institutions. If the intelligence shows a window closing, the people who hold it must be able to act, and the more people who are told, the more leaks, the longer the delay and the greater the danger. The sitting government is still the government. To tell it that its authority is thinner because an election has not produced a majority is to hand the enemy the timing.

The objection has force. The Rambam himself concedes the point. For a milchemet mitzvah, no court is needed, because delay can cost lives. If the threat is truly immediate in that sense, then the king acts, and the argument I am making does not stand in his way. But notice what the objection proves. It proves that a truly immediate threat needs no special process. It does not prove that the claim of immediacy should be exempt from examination, since the whole dispute exists because the claim is contested, and a contested claim is exactly the case in which the classification matters. The answer to the leak problem is not one man's judgment but a smaller, secure forum, such as the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee and the political-security cabinet, which is the structure the Basic Law already provides for. An optional war announced on the strength of "trust me" fails the Rambam's test. A real defensive war needs no permission and will not be delayed by it.

What a republic owes its citizens in the gap

Keep the defenses and the diplomacy running, as Yaakov did. Do not take an irreversible step on the word of one office-holder, whoever that is. Say which kind of war is being contemplated, and why. Keep the question of the uranium, which is physical and permanent, apart from the question of the regime, which is political and uncertain. And have the surviving camp planned before the first step, because Yaakov did.

Covenantal Republicanism rests on the idea that a government's authority comes with obligations to the people it governs. In a caretaker gap that obligation does not pause. It gets heavier, because the people cannot yet point to a government that has the confidence of the Knesset. I would neither freeze nor leap. I would lean on structure more, so that whoever sits in the chair at the end of the weeks can say that the decision was made by the body that owns it, on the evidence, in the open order of gift, prayer and readiness.

May Hashem guard the people of Israel in the days ahead, and give wisdom to everyone who must decide.
