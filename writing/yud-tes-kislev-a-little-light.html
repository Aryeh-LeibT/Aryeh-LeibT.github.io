A Little Light

The release of 19 Kislev 5559, the Rosh Hashanah of Chassidus, and Tanya chapter 12

Sunday 29 and Monday 30 November 2026 (19 and 20 Kislev 5787) · Tanya, Likkutei Amarim · Tehillim 55, 90 to 96

Preface

This booklet is for Yud-Tes Kislev, Sunday, 29 November 2026 (19 Kislev 5787), and for the second day of the Rosh Hashanah of Chassidus, Monday, 30 November 2026 (20 Kislev 5787). It follows the story of the Maggid’s last Shabbat and the Alter Rebbe’s release, which the Vayishlach week booklet began, and it looks ahead to a week in which Chanukah arrives on Friday, 4 December. The theme is the release of the Alter Rebbe, Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, and his Tanya, and in particular the line in Tanya chapter 12 that a little light pushes away a great deal of darkness.

How it is built. Part One tells the day: the facts, the Maggid, the arrest, the release. Part Two is about the Tanya: how the book began, what the cycle is this year, and chapter 12 in some depth. Part Three links the day to the parsha, Vayeshev, and to Chanukah. Part Four goes through the day’s learning: the Hayom Yom of 19 and 20 Kislev, the Rebbe’s letter on the day, and the psalms. Part Five is for the table, with questions and resolutions. The back matter has a list of what is confirmed and what rests on one source, the psalms in full, a glossary and the bibliography.

On the sources. Every fact has a source, and the sources are named in the text and listed in the bibliography. The Chabad.org daily study pages and calendar pages supply the dates, the Hayom Yom and the Tanya schedule. The Alter Rebbe’s own words about the day come from the Hayom Yom, which quotes his letter. The Tanya text is the Kehot translation as hosted on Sefaria, quoted only in short excerpts. The Rebbe’s teachings come from Chabad.org adaptations, and are credited to the translators and compilers. Where a teaching is summarized rather than quoted, the text says so. The psalms are Hebrew from Sefaria (Miqra according to the Masorah) and English from the JPS Tanakh (1985), with the Names of G-d written as Hashem and G-d in English and with ה' and אלקים in Hebrew.

On what is not settled. A few items rest on a single source or could not be checked against the original; the text says so where they appear, and Appendix A lists them.
