The Obligation to the Dead: Vilnius, Memory, and Sacred Ground

Monday 7 September 2026 / 25 Elul 5786 · Internal draft for Writing · VERIFY letter dates and place names

The dead are not a zoning inconvenience.

In early September, Jewish media reported an August letter from U.S. antisemitism envoy Rabbi Yehuda Kaploun to Lithuania’s culture minister Lukas Alsys urging a halt to conference and cultural-center plans on the Piramont / Šnipiškės Jewish cemetery in Vilnius — a burial ground already scarred by Soviet razing, with the Palace of Concerts and Sports sitting on desecrated earth. The Vilna Gaon was originally buried there; his remains were later moved. VERIFY the letter date (reported as 10 August) and the JNS account before treating any detail as courtroom fact. The point for covenantal writing is not a personality fight. It is the claim that memory has standing.

Halacha’s resistance to disturbing graves is not museum aesthetics. It is a refusal to let power rewrite the past by paving it. When a state or developer treats a cemetery as empty land because the community that buried there was murdered, expelled, or administratively erased, domination extends beyond the living. The ledger of the dead is opened and then discarded.

Elul and the approach of Rosh Hashanah sharpen the same pressure. Zikaron — remembrance — is not nostalgia. On the Days of Awe the liturgy binds memory to judgment and kingship. A people that remembers only selectively will crown whatever story flatters it. Sacred ground is one of the places where selective memory becomes concrete: concrete poured over bones.

Pittsburgh grit recognizes the form. You do not “redevelop” a family plot because the paperwork got thin. You check the record, you honor the names, and you refuse the convenient story that nobody important is left to object. Jerusalem emunah adds that Israel’s dead are not orphans of history. The covenant does not expire when the last witness is murdered. Descendants, communities, and honest states still owe the dead a form of non-domination: no arbitrary interference with their rest for the sake of a conference hall’s convenience.

Public argument here should stay precise. Oppose the project on the cemetery’s sacred and historical status. Cite the envoy’s letter as a public diplomatic act, not as a license for contempt toward Lithuanian society as a whole. Many Lithuanians and Jews have worked for memory; the fight is over this ground and this plan. VERIFY names, ministries, and current status of the proposal before any social post that names Kaploun or Alsys — person-aimed posts stay ask-first.

The King is near in Elul. Nearness does not cancel the ledger. It makes the ledger readable. The obligation to the dead is part of that readability. Leave the graves. Tell the truth about what was razed. Build elsewhere.

There is also a geopolitical lesson without campaign theatrics. States that inherit Soviet erasure projects inherit a choice: continue the erasure under cultural language, or reverse it with inconvenient honesty. Conference centers and “revitalization” are not neutral when they sit on Jewish cemeteries. The same non-domination test that applies to Hormuz and to campus procedure applies here: who holds the capacity to interfere with the resting place of a murdered community, and under what law?

Rosh Hashanah’s zikhronot will soon ask heaven and earth to remember. A people that asks to be remembered while paving over its dead is practicing a contradiction. Elul’s near King does not bless that contradiction. Stand with the record. Leave the ground.
