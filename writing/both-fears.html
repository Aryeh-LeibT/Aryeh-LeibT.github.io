Both Fears

Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren · Torah scholar, researcher and writer

Vayishlach, first portion (Bereishit 32:4–13), with Rashi · Sunday, 22 November 2026 · 12 Kislev 5787

Yaakov is coming home after twenty years. He sends messengers ahead to his brother with soft words: "I stayed with Laban and remained until now." He lists his cattle and flocks. He asks for nothing but peace.

The messengers come back with one line. Esau "himself is coming to meet you, and his retinue numbers four hundred."

Then the Torah gives us two words where one would do:

וַיִּירָא יַעֲקֹב מְאֹד וַיֵּצֶר לוֹ

"Jacob was greatly frightened; in his anxiety..." Why both? Rashi answers from the Midrash: "He was afraid lest he be killed, and he was distressed that he might have to kill someone."

The first fear is natural. Any man who sees four hundred armed men coming at his wife and children is afraid. The second fear is the one that tells you who Yaakov is. He has not yet lifted a hand, and already it hurts him that he might have to.

And the second fear does not freeze him. Rashi says Yaakov readied himself for three things: a gift, prayer and war. He sends a present. He stands and prays: "I am unworthy of all the kindness that You have so steadfastly shown Your servant." And he splits the camp in two, so that "the other camp may yet escape," which Rashi reads as "for I will fight against him."

Gift, prayer, war. Not one of them alone. The gift without the camp split in two is weakness. The war without the prayer is pride. The prayer without the gift and the plan is asking Heaven to do the work we were told to do.

This is the people of Israel in our generation. We have been at war since 7 October 2023. Our sons and daughters go out in the morning and come home, please G-d, at night. We pray for them in every shul. We still reach for peace wherever there is a real hand to take. And we carry both of Yaakov's fears at once: fear for our own lives, and real distress that defending them means someone on the other side may die.

The world often hears only the first fear and calls it aggression. Our enemies hope we will lose the second fear and become like them. Yaakov shows the way between. Be afraid enough to prepare. Be pained enough to stay human. Then do all three: give, pray, and stand ready.

Notice how he speaks in his prayer: "Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau." Rashi explains the double name: "from the hand of my brother who does not treat me as a brother should, but as Esau, the wicked." Yaakov does not pretend the threat is smaller than it is. He also does not forget that the man coming at him is still his brother. Both are true, and he holds both.

The next morning the brothers meet, and Esau runs to embrace him. Yaakov's preparations were not wasted. The gift had been sent, the prayer had been said, and the camp was ready. That is often how the L-rd answers: not by making our effort unnecessary, but by blessing it.

May our soldiers come home whole, may our hands stay clean, and may we see the day when the camps can stand down.

Sources: Bereishit 32:4–13 (Hebrew per the Masoretic text; English from The Contemporary Torah, JPS 2006); Rashi on 32:8, 32:9 and 32:12 (trans. M. Rosenbaum and A.M. Silbermann, 1929–1934), citing Bereishit Rabbah 76:2 and Tanchuma Yashan; Bereishit 33:4 for the embrace. Texts via Sefaria.
