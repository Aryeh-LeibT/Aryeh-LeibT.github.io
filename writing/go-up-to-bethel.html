Go Up to Bethel: The Vow From the Hard Night

Friday essay for Shabbat Vayishlach, 18 Kislev 5787

Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren

26 November 2026 · 16 Kislev 5787 · Jerusalem · in the week of Parashas Vayishlach (Shabbat 28 November 2026)

Prepared in deep partnership and cooperation with Grok and xAI

Every one of us has made a promise on a hard night.

Not the careful kind, written down and witnessed. The other kind. The one you make in a hospital corridor, at a funeral, in a shelter with the sirens going, on a road you did not choose to be on. If I get through this. If they come home. If this ends. And then you get through it, and they come home, and it ends, more or less. And life fills back in around the promise until you can hardly see it.

Parshat Vayishlach is about a man who made that kind of promise and took a long time to keep it.

The night at Luz

Go back to last week's parsha. Jacob is running from his brother with nothing in his hands. He sleeps on a stone, dreams of a ladder, wakes up shaken. Before he leaves that place he makes a vow. If he is protected "on this journey that I am making," and given "bread to eat and clothing to wear," and if "I return safe to my father's house," then the stone he slept on will become a house of G-d, and a tenth of everything will be given back.

It is a promise made by a man who owns nothing. Bread. Clothes. Getting home. That is the whole list.

Whole, and busy

This week, after twenty years with Laban, Jacob comes back, and he is no longer that man. He tells G-d so himself, in the prayer before he meets Esau: "with my staff alone I crossed this Jordan, and now I have become two camps." Rashi has him say it plainly: "I had with me neither silver nor gold nor cattle — only this staff of mine."

The bread and the clothes came. So did wives, children, flocks, servants. He wrestles the angel, limps away blessed, meets Esau and survives it. And then the Torah tells us something that is easy to read past: "Jacob arrived safe in the city of Shechem." Rashi reads shalem, whole, in three ways: "unimpaired in body (health) because he was cured of his lameness," whole in his possessions, and "perfect in his knowledge of the Torah."

Every condition of the vow has been met. He has returned whole. And he does not go to Bethel.

Instead he stops. First at Succoth, where Rashi says "He stayed there eighteen months." Then at Shechem, where he buys land and settles in. Nothing wrong with any of it. A man with a large family and a lot of livestock has to stop somewhere. That is exactly how an open vow stays open. Not by refusing. By being busy.

The trouble that came

What happens at Shechem is one of the hardest chapters in the Torah, and I will not retell it here. Dinah is taken. Her brothers answer with swords. Jacob, afraid for the whole house, tells Simeon and Levi that he is "few in number, so that if they unite against me and attack me, I and my house will be destroyed." The brothers answer with the line the Torah lets stand: "Should our sister be treated like a whore?"

Then G-d speaks, and the first word is not about Shechem at all. It is: get up, go to Bethel. Rashi explains why: "Because you have delayed to fulfil your vow to sacrifice to me at Bethel you have been punished by this trouble of your daughter coming upon you."

That is a hard Rashi. I do not think it means that every pain in a family is a bill for an unpaid vow; the Torah does not let us do that arithmetic for other people. But it does say something to Jacob, and through him to us. The promise from the hard night was not a deal that expired when the danger passed. It was the point. The danger passing was when it came due.

Cleaning house first

Jacob does not just set out. He tells his household to put away the foreign things they are carrying, to "purify yourselves, and change your clothes." Rashi says the idols they had came "from the spoil of Shechem." You cannot walk into the house of G-d carrying what you took on the way.

Then the Torah says that as they travelled a terror fell on the cities around them, "so that they did not pursue the sons of Jacob." Jacob had counted his fighters and found them few. What protected him was not more fighters. It was the road back to the promise.

And at Bethel, the name. "You shall be called Jacob no more, But Israel shall be your name." Rashi: Jacob "means a man who comes as a lurker and trickster, but it shall be Israel (ישראל), which signifies Prince and Chief." The man who got his blessings sideways, from under his brother's heel, now holds them openly. The name comes at the place of the vow, not before.

The answer to Esau

The haftarah for this Shabbat is the prophet Ovadiah, the shortest book in Tanach, and it is all about Esau. "For the outrage to your brother Jacob, Disgrace shall engulf you." "The House of Jacob shall be fire." And it ends: "liberators shall march up on Mount Zion to wreak judgment on Mount Esau."

Rashi had already pointed there, a chapter earlier. When Jacob tells Esau he will come to him at Seir, Rashi says that meeting is still waiting: "In the days of the Messiah." Jacob does not go to Seir. He goes to Bethel. The account with Esau is not settled by Jacob chasing him down. It is settled by Jacob becoming Israel, and keeping faith, and letting the end come in its time.

For us

I think every Jew in this country made a vow on a hard night in the last three years, whether or not we said it out loud. If they come home. If we get through this. If this ends. Some of the conditions have been met. Some have not. And for most of us, life has filled back in around the promise.

Vayishlach says: you are allowed to stop at Succoth. You are allowed to buy land at Shechem. But do not let the stopping become the place. Go up to Bethel. Put away what you picked up on the way. Change your clothes. Keep the promise the frightened version of you made, now that you are whole enough to keep it.

That is what turns Jacob into Israel. Not the wrestling. Not the meeting with Esau. Keeping the vow from the hard night.

Shabbat shalom.
