The Infrastructure of Hatred

Internal draft — VERIFY source and policy details before publication

Guards around a shul are necessary. They are not a policy. A fence can keep a gunman away from a door, but it cannot explain who financed the sermon that celebrated the gunman, which institution normalized the claim, which platform carried it, or why the street treated terror as glory. Security is a duty. It is not the whole answer to antisemitic violence.

The Conference of European Rabbis, in a seven-point manifesto reported under the title A Call to End the Violent Hatred, puts that distinction on the table. The source note attributes to the manifesto proposals to certify religious leaders, cut extremist money, oversee institutions that radicalize, defend digital space, and stop the public glorification of terror. The Algemeiner report is the pointer for this draft; the exact wording, authorship, implementation mechanisms, and current status should be verified against the original and the publication before citation.

The argument is direct: hatred has infrastructure. It moves through funding, pulpits, classrooms, organizations, streets, and screens. It can be preached, subsidized, rehearsed, clipped, translated, and rewarded. If the state responds only by placing more concrete outside Jewish buildings, it protects one address while leaving the system that selects the next target intact. Security without diagnosis becomes a longer fence.

That does not make the fence contemptible. A Jewish school needs a secure entrance. A synagogue needs trained protection. Families need the state to treat threats as threats before they become funerals. Anyone who skips that duty in the name of a more ambitious theory is asking vulnerable people to subsidize his eloquence. The covenant begins with preserving life.

But preserving life also requires naming the conditions that place it at risk. The manifesto’s emphasis on certification is not a demand that the state become the author of theology. It is a demand that religious authority not be a blank check for incitement, recruitment, or the laundering of violence. Cutting extremist money is not a claim that every foreign donation is criminal. It is a request for transparency and enforceable standards where money sustains institutions that radicalize. Defending digital space is not censorship by another name. It means asking which platforms amplify threats, how networks coordinate, and whether rules are applied when Jews are the target.

Street glorification matters because public ritual teaches. When terror is celebrated in public, children learn that violence can produce honor. Jews then receive the lesson in reverse: hide the star, avoid the crowd, accept the fence. A serious policy must break that pedagogy, not merely escort its victims through it.

Pittsburgh knows what it means when a house of prayer is treated as a soft target. Jerusalem knows that Jewish life cannot be organized forever around the expectation that the next attack is inevitable. The answer is neither panic nor fantasy. It is a layered civic covenant: protect the building, investigate the threat, expose the funding, regulate the institution through lawful due process, challenge the platform, and insist that public authorities name the hatred clearly.

There are cautions. “Infrastructure of hatred” must not become a license for collective suspicion or for punishing people for an identity, accent, or unpopular belief. The target is conduct and enabling structure: incitement, terrorist glorification, recruitment, clandestine financing, and institutional failure. Due process is not a luxury reserved for comfortable times. It is part of the protection that keeps security from becoming domination.

Nor should Jewish communities be told that a manifesto is a substitute for political work. A seven-point plan is a beginning. It needs measurable definitions, competent oversight, appeal rights, cooperation across borders, and a way to distinguish dangerous radicalization from ordinary religious disagreement. It needs officials willing to enforce the law when the crowd is large and the slogans are fashionable. It needs technology companies to stop treating antisemitic threats as merely offensive content.

The central test is simple. Will Europe ask only how to guard Jews after hatred has matured, or will it ask how hatred was financed, preached, distributed, and celebrated before it reached the gate? A fence is visible. Infrastructure is harder: accounts, channels, lecturers, algorithms, associations, and municipal permissions. It is precisely because the work is harder that it is the work.

We should hold both truths without flinching. Security guards are necessary. Security guards are not a policy. The policy is to defend Jewish life while dismantling the networks that teach others to hate it. That is not vengeance. It is public accountability, and it is the minimum a covenantal society owes its children.
