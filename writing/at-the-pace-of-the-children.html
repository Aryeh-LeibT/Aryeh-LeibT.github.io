At the Pace of the Children: Why Yaakov Turned Down Esav's Escort

Vayishlach, the Haftarah of Ovadiah, and a treaty with Rome that the Ramban never forgot

Aryeh Leib Thorne Toren

25 November 2026 · 15 Kislev 5787 · Jerusalem · in the week of Parashas Vayishlach (Shabbat 28 November 2026)

Prepared in deep partnership and cooperation with Grok and xAI

Two offers on the road

Today's section of the weekly portion, the fourth reading of Vayishlach, is Bereishit 33:6 to 33:20 (Sefaria daily calendar for 25 November 2026, https://www.sefaria.org/api/calendars?year=2026&month=11&day=25, opened 25 November 2026). The meeting with Esav is over. The brothers have embraced and wept. And then Esav makes two offers that sound like the happy ending.

The first: "Let us start on our journey, and I will proceed at your pace" (Bereishit 33:12). The second, after Yaakov declines the first: "Let me assign to you some of the people who are with me" (33:15). Yaakov turns down both. His stated reason is plain and practical: "My lord knows that the children are frail and that the flocks and herds, which are nursing, are a care to me; if they are driven hard a single day, all the flocks will die. Let my lord go on ahead of his servant, while I travel slowly, at the pace of the cattle before me and at the pace of the children, until I come to my lord in Seir" (33:13 to 33:14; JPS translation on Sefaria, https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.33.12-17, opened 25 November 2026).

Esav goes back to Seir. Yaakov goes on to Sukkot, builds a house and makes shelters for his cattle (33:16 to 33:17).

The scene is short, and it is easy to read it as good manners between two brothers who have just made peace. Our Sages read it as something more: a lesson in how a small people travels next to a great power.

What Rashi hears in "until I come to my lord in Seir"

Rashi notices that Yaakov names a destination he has no plan to reach. Citing the Talmud (Avodah Zarah 25b), he writes that Yaakov "mentioned a much longer journey, for he really intended to go only as far as Succoth." Yaakov's thought, in Rashi's words: "If he means to do me harm let him wait to do so until I reach his abode at Seir." So when will Yaakov go up to Seir? "In the days of the Messiah, as it is said (Obadiah 1:21) 'And saviours shall go up on Mount Zion to judge the mount of Esau'" (Rashi on Bereishit 33:14, Rosenbaum and Silbermann translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Genesis.33.14, opened 25 November 2026).

That verse is the last line of this Shabbat's haftarah. The haftarah of Vayishlach is the whole book of Ovadiah, 1:1 to 1:21 (Sefaria daily calendar, above), and it closes: "For liberators shall march up on Mount Zion to wreak judgment on Mount Esau; and dominion shall be [Hashem's]" (Ovadiah 1:21, JPS, divine name written here as Hashem, https://www.sefaria.org/Obadiah.1.21, opened 25 November 2026). The Sages who chose the haftarah and Rashi reading 33:14 are pointing at the same horizon. Yaakov does not refuse Esav forever. He refuses to make the trip on Esav's schedule, under Esav's guard, before its time.

Rashi also slows down on the word le-iti, "slowly." He says it means "my slowness," from a root of ease and gentleness, and that "according to the pace of the children" means "according to the speed which they are able to keep up" (Rashi on 33:14, above). The pace of the whole camp is set by the ones who cannot be hurried.

Rabbeinu HaKadosh reads the parsha before going to Rome

The Midrash turns the second offer, the armed escort, into a rule of statecraft. On "Let me assign to you some of the people who are with me," Bereishit Rabbah says: "he sought to accompany him, but he did not accept it." Then it tells a story about Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi, called Rabbeinu: "when he would ascend to the empire, he would look at this portion and would not take a Roman with him. One time, he did not look at it, and he took Romans with him. He did not reach Akko before he sold his horse" (Bereishit Rabbah 78:15, Sefaria Midrash Rabbah translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Bereishit_Rabbah.78.15, opened 25 November 2026).

Rabbeinu did not avoid Rome. The Midrash takes his journeys for granted; "when he would ascend to the empire" describes a habit. He went as a guest who kept his own arrangements, and that the one time he let Rome supply his escort, the escort cost him.

The Ramban: a covenant with Rome

The Ramban carries the lesson into Jewish history. At the opening of the parsha, where Yaakov sends messengers to Esav, he first sets out the general rule: "everything that happened to our father with his brother Esau will constantly occur to us with Esau's children," and so we prepare as Yaakov did, "for prayer, for giving him a present, and for rescue by methods of warfare." Then he quotes the Sages' criticism that Yaakov should not have sent messengers to an Esav who "was going his way," and adds his own reading: "In my opinion this too hints at the fact that we instigated our falling into the hand of Edom [Rome] for the Hasmonean kings during the period of the Second Temple entered into a covenant with the Romans, and some of them even went to Rome to seek an alliance. This was the cause of their falling into the hands of the Romans" (Ramban on Bereishit 32:4, Chavel translation, https://www.sefaria.org/Ramban_on_Genesis.32.4, opened 25 November 2026).

The covenant the Ramban means is on record. The First Book of Maccabees, chapter 8, tells how Yehudah the Maccabee "had heard of the fame of the Romans," that "with their friends and such as relied upon them they kept amity," and how he sent two envoys to Rome "to make a league of amity and confederacy with them, and to intreat them that they would take the yoke from them; for they saw that the kingdom of the Grecians did oppress Israel with servitude." The Senate's reply, the book says, was written "in tables of brass" and sent to Jerusalem, with each side pledged to help the other in war (1 Maccabees 8, Brenton translation, https://www.sefaria.org/The_Book_of_Maccabees_I.8, opened 25 November 2026).

The same chapter shows why the offer was so attractive. It admires Rome's senate of 320 men "consulting alway for the people" and reports that "whom they would help to a kingdom, those reign; and whom again they would, they displace" (1 Maccabees 8, above). That second line is the warning hidden inside the praise. A power that can make kings can unmake them.

Chanukah begins this year on the evening of Friday 4 December 2026 (Hebcal, https://www.hebcal.com, opened 25 November 2026). The story of the Maccabees' courage is the story we will tell. The Ramban asks us also to remember the treaty that came after the victory.

The strongest objection, and an answer

The weakest link, named first. The Ramban's reading is one opinion, and the historian of the period does not say the same thing. Josephus, describing Pompey's capture of Jerusalem in the consulship of Gaius Antonius and Marcus Tullius Cicero, puts the blame on a civil war inside the royal house: "Now the occasions of this misery which came upon Jerusalem were Hyrcanus and Aristobulus, by raising a sedition one against the other; for now we lost our liberty, and became subject to the Romans" (Josephus, Antiquities 14.4.3 to 14.4.5, Whiston translation, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Antiquities_of_the_Jews/Book_XIV, opened 25 November 2026). A modern summary dates that siege to 63 BCE and says Pompey "had been asked to intervene in a dispute over succession to the Hasmonean throne," and that his conquest "spelled the end of an independent Jewish state" (Wikipedia, "Siege of Jerusalem (63 BC)," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(63_BC), opened 25 November 2026). There is also a century between Yehudah's treaty and Pompey's arrival. So was it the alliance or the infighting?

The two readings fit together better than they first appear. Josephus and the modern summary agree that Rome came in because the two brothers asked it to settle their quarrel. That is exactly the habit the Ramban is describing: a ruling house that had grown used to treating Rome as the referee. The first treaty asked Rome to lift the Greek yoke. A century later, the heirs asked Rome to choose between them. The alliance did not doom anyone by itself; it made outside rescue the first answer, and a divided house then reached for it. Rabbeinu's story says the same thing at the scale of one traveler: the danger is not the meeting with Rome but letting Rome provide the guard.

A second objection: the plain text gives a farming reason, nursing animals and small children, not a political one. That is true, and it is the heart of the lesson. Yaakov's politics come out of his household. He declines the faster pace because the weakest in his camp cannot keep it, and he declines the escort because a guard who answers to someone else sets someone else's pace.

For our day

The Ramban's rule cuts in two directions, and it should be read in both. It does not say "refuse every friend." Yaakov sends gifts, speaks with courtesy, and calls Esav "my lord." Rabbeinu goes to Rome. The Maccabees' envoys were not traitors; they were trying to lift a real yoke. What the parsha warns against is a particular kind of dependence: letting another power set the pace of your journey, supply the guard at your side, or decide your internal quarrels for you.

A covenantal republic, a people bound first by its covenant with G-d and only then by its agreements with others, can hold strong friendships and still keep its own pace. It can thank a great ally and still say, as Yaakov did, "Let my lord go on ahead." It can accept help and still build its own shelters at Sukkot. And when it is tempted to settle a fight at home by inviting a stronger hand from outside, it can remember that the last time we did that, we lost the kingdom for a very long time.

The test Yaakov uses is the gentlest one in the Torah: the pace of the children. Policy that runs faster than the weakest in the camp can bear looks like strength for a day, and then, in Yaakov's words, "all the flocks will die."
